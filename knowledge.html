01 / Historical source

Oscilloscope Testing of the Audio Amplifier

G. F. Griffiths · The Radio Constructor · June 1968. Preserved by the National Valve Museum.

Read the original article and its circuit / waveform figures ↗

Griffiths explains how harmonics reshape a sine wave, then traces faults through a 10 W valve amplifier and interprets square-wave responses. His demonstration combines a transformer-derived 50 Hz, 6.3 V signal with about 0.6 V of second harmonic (roughly 10%). The oscillator spans 90–410 Hz; a 5–50 kΩ potentiometer controls injection. Examples use 100, 150 and 200 Hz harmonics. He notes that distortion below roughly 5% can be hard to see.

The amplifier checks start at the output-transformer secondary and work back through the signal path. Points 11–16 are bypassed nodes; 17–18 also carry feedback, and point 19 is the HT reservoir. For general square-wave checks he suggests about 400 Hz and first checking the generator directly on the scope.

Source synopsis only; the complete article and original figures remain at the link above. The guide below is a newly written RIOT adaptation. Proposed settings and illustrative waveforms are not measurements of a RIOT project.

02 / Modern adaptation

Make the measurement trustworthy first

A digital storage oscilloscope adds repeatable triggering, cursors, saved captures and, on some models, FFT analysis. A clean-looking trace still does not establish low distortion. Establish the generator, probe and load conditions before interpreting the amplifier.

Grounding changes the circuit. On a conventional earth-referenced bench scope, the ground clip is not a free choice of reference. Never defeat protective earth. A floating or high-voltage measurement needs an appropriately rated differential/isolated measurement system. [S2]
  1. Map returns with power off. Confirm the scope, generator and DUT grounding arrangement. Treat BNC shells and channel returns as common unless the exact manufacturer documentation proves otherwise. Battery power alone does not establish isolation; USB or a charger can restore an earth path. Do not clip a ground lead onto a negative supply rail, internal VREF, bridged output, valve anode or another driven node.
  2. Use the correct load. Disconnect loudspeakers for commissioning. A power amplifier needs a suitably rated resistive dummy load and cooling; choose the impedance from its design. Keep a valve output transformer correctly loaded during powered signal tests. A crossover or preamp needs a representative amplifier input load, not an 8 Ω power load.
  3. Check ratings, then compensate the probes. Use compatible ×10 probes as a starting point, match each channel’s attenuation setting and compensate on the scope’s calibration output. Use short returns for fast signals. Probe/input voltage limits, common-mode ratings and frequency derating apply. [S3]
  4. Establish DC before AC. Check supply, reference and output DC with a DMM against the project’s limits. Start the scope DC-coupled. AC coupling can help display small ripple but hides offset and can alter low-frequency waveform shape; it does not make an unsafe voltage safe.
  5. Verify the generator at the DUT input. Begin with a low-level 1 kHz sine. A proposed initial value is 100 mV RMS, reduced further for a high-gain or uncommissioned stage. Check actual amplitude and offset on CH1: a generator’s 50 Ω / high-impedance amplitude convention may otherwise mislead you.

03 / Bench sequence

Input, output, then the stage in between

  1. Capture the source alone. Save the generator waveform at the intended input level and loading. Keep this as the comparison when a later trace looks distorted.
  2. Measure gain at low level. Capture input and output simultaneously. Use the same amplitude convention: Av = Vout / Vin, and gain(dB) = 20 log10(Av). Identify inversion separately. Compare 100 Hz, 1 kHz and 10 kHz only where the circuit is intended to pass them.
  3. Find where distortion starts. If the output is wrong but CH1 is clean, compare adjacent accessible signal nodes at unchanged input, load and supply. Preserve DC operating conditions while probing. A trace shape narrows the investigation; it does not uniquely identify a faulty part.
  4. Check level dependence. Increase a sine gradually only after bias, DC and thermal checks pass. Record the last visually unclipped output and the first visible clipping, with test duration and temperature. This is a scope-observed clipping boundary, not a certified THD power rating. Back off promptly.
  5. Measure response deliberately. Hold measured input constant while stepping frequency; log output/input ratio and phase. For phase, |φ| = 360° × |Δt| / T; note whether output leads or lags. Include probe/channel timing errors when the delay is small.
  6. Inspect ripple and instability separately. A long timebase can reveal 50/100 Hz hum or supply ripple; a shorter capture is needed for HF bursts. Compare the source, supply, input and output before assigning cause. Move probes with power off where access makes slipping possible.
Resistive-load example — calculation, not a RIOT result
A clean sine of 20 V peak-to-peak has VRMS = 20 / (2√2) = 7.07 V. Across a measured 8 Ω resistor, P ≈ VRMS²/R = 6.25 W.

The Vpp-to-RMS conversion applies only to a sine. For a clipped or arbitrary waveform, use a valid true-RMS measurement over a suitable record. A reactive loudspeaker is not the resistor in this calculation.

04 / Visual diagnosis

Recognise the symptom. Confirm the cause.

All six drawings below are mathematical illustrations generated for this guide. They are not scope captures, simulated RIOT circuits or evidence of any build’s performance.

Small-signal square-wave check

After sine-wave checks pass, try a small 400 Hz square wave into the appropriate dummy load. First capture the generator directly using the same probes. Do not begin at full rated output: fast edges and transformer volt-seconds can stress an amplifier differently from a mid-band sine. Higher-frequency tests are optional investigations, not universal acceptance gates. Measure ringing with cursors: fring ≈ 1 / Tring. Overshoot can be recorded as 100 × (Vpeak − Vplateau) / step amplitude, with the reference levels stated.

05 / Digital additions

FFT shows what the sine trace hides

Use FFT if the scope supports it. Compare the generator baseline and amplifier output at controlled level and loading. Peaks at 2f, 3f and beyond can reveal harmonics; hum, interference and aliases require separate identification. [S4]

  • Capture length and rate work together. For N samples at rate fs, the record duration is N/fs and FFT bin spacing is fs/N. Example: 100,000 samples at 1 MS/s gives 100 ms and 10 Hz bin spacing. This is a calculated example, not an instrument specification. Windowing broadens spectral features, so bin spacing is not the complete resolution specification. [S5]
  • Avoid false harmonics. Keep significant input content below Nyquist (fs/2), with practical margin; increasing time span may reduce the actual sample rate. Reacquire at a higher sample rate and inspect whether peaks move. A square wave contains substantial high-frequency content that can alias. [S4]
  • Record the window. A Hann window is a useful general starting choice where available. A coherent integer-cycle record can reduce leakage; rectangular and flat-top windows have different trade-offs. Do not compare amplitudes obtained with unknown window corrections. [S5]
  • Read relative levels consistently. A harmonic at −40 dBc is 1% of the fundamental’s voltage amplitude; −60 dBc is 0.1%. For compatible RMS harmonic amplitudes, THD = √(V2² + V3² + …) / V1. Declare the harmonic range and bandwidth. THD+N additionally includes noise.

An FFT display does not establish a precision distortion floor. Generator distortion, ADC noise, vertical range, probe scaling, windowing and bandwidth all contribute. Use a suitably characterised audio analyser/interface chain for credible low-level THD+N results. A PC audio input needs its own protected, calibrated attenuation interface; do not connect a power-amplifier output directly to it.

The harmonics experiment, updated

For a low-voltage learning exercise, create a waveform mathematically or in a suitable arbitrary generator: v(t) = A sin(2πft) + 0.1A sin(4πft + θ). At f = 1 kHz, the second term is a 2 kHz harmonic at −20 dBc. Vary θ to see how shape changes while the amplitude ratio stays fixed. Add a 3 kHz term separately for comparison. Do not wire two generator outputs together: use a documented summing circuit or a generator that can synthesise the combined waveform. No mains-derived reference is needed.

07 / Evidence that can be reused

Save the conditions with the trace

Save a screen capture plus waveform CSV/raw data when supported. A screenshot preserves context; samples allow later analysis. Record:

  • Project, board/channel, revision, date, test point and return reference; schematic node identity.
  • Supply voltage/current limit, measured DC/bias, load impedance and power rating, warm-up time and temperature.
  • Source frequency, actual input amplitude and offset; output amplitude, gain and observed clipping boundary.
  • Probe type/attenuation, input coupling, input impedance, V/div, time/div, trigger, bandwidth limit, sample rate, record length and acquisition mode.
  • For FFT: window, scaling, harmonic range/bandwidth and generator baseline. For phase: reference channel and lead/lag convention.
